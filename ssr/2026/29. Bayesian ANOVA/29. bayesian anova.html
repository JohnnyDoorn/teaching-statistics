Bayesian parameter estimation

Beer tasting

  • \(\theta\) is the probability of a correct identification

Bayesian updating

Bayes’ rule for knowledge updating

\[\large \underbrace{P(\theta \mid data)}_{\text{Posterior beliefs}} = \underbrace{P(\theta)}_{\text{Prior beliefs}} \times \overbrace{\underbrace{\frac{P(data \mid \theta)}{P(data)}}_{\substack{\text{Average prediction} \\ \text{across all } \theta\text{'s}}}}^{\text{Prediction for specific } \theta}\]

Prior distribution - (\(P(\theta)\))

Each model has assigned a prior probability distribution to the parameter \(\theta\) by means of a probability distribution. In the case of a proportion, a convenient distribution is the Beta distribution because it also has a domain of [0,1].

Now what is the data saying

Ten tasters

\(\begin{aligned} k &= 8 \\ n &= 10 \end{aligned}\)

k = 8
n = 10

Likelihood function - \(P(data | \theta)\)

How likely is 8 out of 10 for all possible \(\theta\) values?

\[\binom{n}{k} \theta^k (1-\theta)^{n-k}\]

Marginal likelihood - \(P(data)\)

How likely is 8 out of 10 for all \(\theta\) values in the model, on average?

Posterior

Now we can update Alex’s belief about the possible values of theta based on the data (the likelihood function) we found. Values with a likelihood higher than the average likelihood receive a boost, and others receive a penalty, in plausibility.

Updating beliefs

Posterior distribution - \(P(\theta | data)\)

Bayesian estimation

The posterior distribution is a probability distribution, so we can make probabilistic statements based on it:

In the Bayesian framework, this is known as the credible interval. This interval entails taking the middle \(x\)% of the posterior distribution. For instance, we can take a 95% credible interval, which ranges from \(0.482\) to \(0.940\) in this case. This means that under Alex’s model, there is a 95% probability that the true value of \(\theta\) is between \(0.482\) and \(0.940\).

Take home message

  • Bayesians quantify uncertainty through distributions.
  • The more peaked the distribution, the lower the uncertainty.
  • Incoming information continually updates our knowledge; today’s posterior is tomorrow’s prior.

Bayesian hypothesis testing

Bayesian Hypothesis Testing

  • \(H_0\), the null hypothesis. For instance \(\theta = .5\) (people cannot taste the difference between alcoholic and non-alcoholic beer).
  • \(H_A\) is the hypothesis that relaxes the restriction imposed by \(H_0\), and instead considers multiple values for \(\theta\).

Prior Belief

\[\large \underbrace{\frac{P(H_A \mid data)}{P(H_0 \mid data)}}_\textrm{Posterior odds} = \underbrace{\frac{P(H_A)}{P(H_0)}}_\textrm{Prior odds} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(data \mid H_A)}{P(data \mid H_0)}}_\textrm{Bayes Factor}\]

Bayes Factor

\[\underbrace{\frac{P(data \mid H_A)}{P(data \mid H_0)}}_\textrm{Bayes Factor}\] A ratio of the marginal likelihood of the data for the alternative and the null models.

A Bayes factor of \({BF}_{10} = 3\), means that the data are 3 times more likely under the alternative model than under the null model.

Bayes Factor

  • Sarah’s model has a marginal likelihood of 0.0439 for 8 correct identifications
  • Alex’s model has a marginal likelihood of 0.0909 for 8 correct identifications
  • \(\text{BF}_{SA} =\) 0.0439 / 0.0909 = 0.48
  • The data are 0.48 times more likely under Sarah’s model than under Alex’s model
  • The data are 2.07 times more likely under Alex’s model than under Sarah’s model

Advantages of the Bayes Factor

  • Provides a continuous degree of evidence without requiring an all-or-none decision.
  • Allows evidence to be monitored during data collection.
  • Differentiates between “the data support H0” (evidence for absence) and “the data are not informative” (absence of evidence).

Metrics we have used

Question Metrics
Do we have evidence for an effect? \(p\)-value (\(t\), \(F\), \(\chi^2\)), \(\text{BF}_{10}\)
Which model predicted the data the best? \(R^2\)/\(F\) change + \(p\), adjusted \(R^2\), \(\text{BF}_{10}\), \(\text{BF}_{\text{incl}}\)
How big is the effect? \(d\), \(r\), \(R^2\), \(\omega^2\), rank-biserial \(r\), odds ratio
How precise is this effect estimate? Standard error, 95% CI, 95% credible interval

Alternatives we have not discussed (not exam material)

  • Equivalence testing
    • Is the effect small enough to ignore? (Lakens et al., 2018)
  • \(p\)-value as evidence, without \(\alpha\) (Fisher’s original idea)
    • Smaller \(p\), stronger evidence against \(H_0\)
    • Common in practice
  • AIC / BIC
    • Model fit with a penalty for complexity
    • BIC approximates the Bayes factor
  • Multiverse analysis
    • What if we had made other choices? (Steegen et al., 2016)

Exam note: moderation (lecture 26)

  • Keep the default settings of each analysis, no need to center by hand
    • Process module centers for you (Mean-centered moderation)
    • Linear regression does not center
  • So \(b_1\) and \(b_2\) differ between the two analyses
    • Process gives the effect at the mean of the other predictor
    • Linear regression gives the effect when the other predictor is 0
  • The interaction \(b_3\) is the same in both
  • Check the assumptions in Linear Regression, not in the Process module
    • VIF in the model without the interaction
    • Checking the assumptions (lecture 21)

Exam note: chi-squared test (lecture 28)

  • Fisher’s exact test when an expected count is below 5 (Cells → Expected)
    • In JASP via Statistics → Odds ratio (2x2 only)
  • No Yates’s correction (χ² continuity correction)
  • Odds ratio from the observed counts
    • The question tells you which odds go on top
    • JASP gives the log odds ratio by default (Log Odds Ratio)
